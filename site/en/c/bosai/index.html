Disaster and emergency

23 drawings in Disaster and emergency, each generated from a single YAML file.

  • Escape routes on a third floor: two ways out of every room to a staircase, with the travel distance and the fire doors and extinguishers along the way
  • Fireworks safety distances, a circle per shell size
  • Shelter layout: 3.5 sq m a person, and how many toilets
  • Crowd plan: how many can flow out against how many come in
  • Extinguishers and hose reels, measured two different ways
  • Sprinkler layout: area per head and spacing, decided together
  • Egress plan: three lengths answering three questions
  • Fire alarm devices: spacing is a circle, not a square
  • Not a picture of the fire - the way out is written on it
  • A typhoon forecast: the circle is where the centre lands 70% of the time
  • A surface chart: the barbs tell you which way the front is moving
  • Fire compartments: the shortcut you use every day is the one that closes — a drill map must show the route after the shutters come down, not before
  • Deciding whether to leave: five questions answered once, in advance — and flood-depth bands read as floors of your own house, not as metres
  • A single-storey nursery has no upstairs, so the plan is half about the building next door — and three people coming out to help halves the time the children spend outside
  • Damage certification: a hazard map measures from the ground, this measures from the floor — so the same water gives two different numbers, and 0.1m changes the category
  • Disaster toilets are counted in uses, not litres: five per person per day, so a family of four needs 140 bags for a week — and the bags stay in the house
  • START triage: four things to look at, three separate roads to red, and only two treatments allowed on the spot — because stopping costs the people behind
  • Disaster headquarters: one chain of command and one place for information, and for the biggest events the report skips a rung and goes to prefecture and nation at once
  • Opening a shelter starts by checking whether the building itself survived — put people in first and you may have to move them again
  • A red placard does not mean total loss: it answers whether it is safe to go in now, not how badly the building is damaged — two different papers, two different questions
  • Knots are chosen by the job they do — joining two ropes, tying to an object, or stopping an end — and every knot is the weakest point of the rope, taking it down to 40-80% of its rated strength
  • Intensity and magnitude measure different things — one earthquake has a single magnitude but as many intensities as there are places — and because each step of magnitude multiplies the energy by about 32, two steps is already a thousandfold, which is exactly what the small-looking numbers hide
  • An earthquake early warning does not predict anything; it borrows the gap between the fast P wave and the slow S wave. Three seconds of waveform are needed to size the quake and the processing takes seconds more, and those costs are the same however far away the epicentre is — so the warning time is only what is left over, and directly beneath a shallow quake there is nothing left.
