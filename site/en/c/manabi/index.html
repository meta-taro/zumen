Learning and geometry

22 drawings in Learning and geometry, each generated from a single YAML file.

  • Ray tracing through a convex lens: there are only three lines to draw, and two of them fix the image
  • A football is a truncated icosahedron: the 12 degrees missing at every vertex are what make it a sphere
  • Braille has only six dots, so “a” and “1” are the same shape until a number sign says otherwise
  • Slope notation: roofs in sun, ramps in fractions, roads in percent, railways in per mille — degrees are the minority
  • Isometric, third-angle and oblique: one scale serves all three axes, but circles become ellipses
  • Honeycomb cells: for a given area the hexagon has the shortest wall of any shape that tiles
  • Ruler-and-compass constructions: no scale is used, because equal compass openings make equal lengths
  • Figures in maths problems: the marks carry the meaning — equal ticks, right-angle squares, parallel chevrons — and only the given values are written on the figure
  • Two set squares come as a pair because two of them make ten angles in 15-degree steps, where one alone makes only four
  • The shape of the periodic table is the electron configuration: block widths of 2, 6, 10 and 14 are orbital capacities, so no other width is possible
  • A spider does not stick to its own web because only the spiral is sticky: the radials and the temporary scaffold are dry, and the scaffold is cut away as the sticky spiral is laid from the outside in
  • A population pyramid is not a picture of today but eighty years of births stacked upright: the bulges and the notch move up one step every year, and the notch at 1966 records a year people avoided, not a generation that was lost
  • Two angles per residue are almost the whole story of a protein backbone: the peptide bond is a partial double bond so omega barely turns, leaving phi and psi, and each secondary structure lands in its own patch of the plot — which is why structure validation is done on six separate plots, since glycine and proline are allowed quite different places
  • Throw away every length and only the fold is left: a TOPS cartoon draws helices as circles and strands as triangles, and whether a connecting line stops at the edge of a symbol or runs into its centre is what tells you which end of the strand it joins — so two proteins of very different size can share one diagram
  • Diffraction records only intensities, never phases, so a crystallographer has to fetch the phases from somewhere else — a known structure, anomalous scattering, or a heavy-atom derivative — whereas cryo-EM has no phase problem at all and pays instead by having to average tens of thousands of particles
  • Three orderings that never agree: the 50 yen coin is smaller than the 5, the 10 is bigger than the 100, and the weights rank differently again — the only two with holes are the 5 and the 50, which are then told apart by the milled edge, because the set is designed to be read by hand
  • Only the middle two fields of an ISBN change length, so a publisher that issues many books holds a short registrant number and gets a longer title field in return — and the check digit, a weighted sum of alternating 1s and 3s, is deliberately imperfect: swapping two adjacent digits that differ by five slips straight through
  • The whole A series follows from two decisions — A0 has an area of exactly one square metre and the sides are in the ratio root two — and root two is the only ratio that survives being halved, which is also why a photocopier needs just one number to step between sizes
  • At a quarter past three the hands are not together: while the minute hand travelled ninety degrees the hour hand moved seven and a half, and the same arithmetic explains why the hands coincide eleven times in twelve hours rather than twelve
  • Ninety-seven leap years every four hundred, not a hundred: dropping three of them brings the average year to 365.2425 days, within 0.0003 of the real one — and the four-hundred-year total happens to divide by seven, so the weekdays come back round as well
  • Electron pairs around a central atom push each other as far apart as they can, and that alone fixes the shape. Methane, ammonia and water all have four pairs on the same tetrahedral skeleton; what differs is how many of them are bonds, and each lone pair squeezes the bond angle further — 109.5, then 107, then 104.5 degrees.
  • Brightness is set not by the aperture or the shutter but by their sum, so equal exposures line up on a diagonal: f/2.8 at 1/500, f/4 at 1/250 and f/5.6 at 1/125 are all 12 EV. One stop either way halves the light. What the diagonal does not equalise is the picture — a smaller aperture deepens the focus and a slower shutter lets movement blur, which is why choosing along it is the craft.
